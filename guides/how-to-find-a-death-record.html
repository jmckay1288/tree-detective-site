How to Find a Death Record for an Ancestor

A death record is often the single richest document in a family tree: it can name a person's parents, spouse, birthplace, occupation, and burial place on one page. It is also, for many researchers, the record they cannot find. Knowing how to find a death record is mostly a matter of knowing where deaths were written down, in what order to look, and what to try when the obvious document doesn't exist.

Start with the last place you saw them alive

Before searching for a death, pin down the window. Find your ancestor's last certain appearance — a census, a city directory, a child's marriage where they stood as a witness, a land sale. Then find the first record where they are gone: a spouse listed as widowed, an estate being settled, a widow remarrying. Your search happens inside that window, in the county where they last lived. Guessing a year and searching an entire state is how people walk right past records that were there all along.

Death certificates: state and county records

Modern death certificates are government records, kept by states — and for earlier years, often by counties. Statewide registration began at different times in different states: a few started in the 1800s, many in the early 1900s, and compliance took years to become complete, so a death in the first years of a system may simply never have been registered. Who may order a certificate also varies by state and by how recent the death is — many states restrict recent certificates to close family, while older ones are open to any researcher or have moved to the state archives. Fees and procedures differ too, so check the rules for the state and year you need rather than assuming. And one caution about the prize itself: the personal details on a certificate came from an informant, often a grieving relative, so the parents' names it gives are only as accurate as that person's memory.

Search the indexes first

Don't order anything until you have searched the indexes. Many states publish death indexes covering their certificate files, and the Social Security Death Index is a fast check for people who died from the 1960s onward and had Social Security numbers — it gives a death date and last residence, which is often enough to point you at the right county. Cemetery and burial indexes, and the big record sites' death collections, round out the first pass. An index hit gives you the exact date or certificate number that makes getting the record itself quick and targeted instead of a fishing trip.

When there is no certificate: obituaries, cemeteries, and probate

For deaths before statewide registration — or when the certificate can't be found — work the alternates, the same ones our record search services use every day. Obituaries and death notices in local newspapers often give the death date, family members, church, and burial place. Cemetery records and headstones can supply dates for which no document survives. Probate is the sleeper: an estate file is usually opened within weeks of a death, and wills, administrations, and inventories can date a death closely even when nothing else does. Church burial registers, funeral home records, and coroner's files — for sudden or unattended deaths — complete the net.

Narrow the window with indirect evidence

If the death date itself is what you're missing, census clues help. A wife listed as "widowed" in 1900 brackets her husband's death before that census. A husband appearing with a new wife brackets both a death and a remarriage. City directories sometimes mark a man as deceased, or begin listing his widow under her own name, in the year after his death. Each clue shrinks the window until the record search becomes manageable — and a narrow, well-evidenced search is what separates a found death record from a hopeless one.
