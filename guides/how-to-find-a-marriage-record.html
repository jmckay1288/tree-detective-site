How to Find a Marriage Record for an Ancestor

A marriage record does something few other records can: it joins two families on one document, and on later records it may name four parents at once. It is also one of the most mis-searched records in genealogy, because marriages were usually recorded somewhere different from where most people look first. Knowing how to find a marriage record means knowing which office kept it, what form it took in that era, and which county to ask.

Marriage records live at the county level

Unlike modern birth and death certificates, which are state records, marriages in the United States were traditionally a county matter. A couple applied for a license at the county courthouse, the officiant performed the ceremony, and the completed paperwork went back to the county to be filed. Depending on the place and period, the surviving record may be a license (the permission to marry), a register entry (a line in the county's marriage book), a bond (an older form, common in the South and mid-Atlantic into the 1800s, where a bondsman — often a relative of the bride — guaranteed there was no legal obstacle), or a minister's return (the officiant's report back to the clerk, sometimes the only proof the ceremony actually happened). A license alone shows a couple intended to marry; the return or register entry is what confirms they did.

What a marriage record typically shows

Early records can be sparse — a bond or register line may give only the two names and a date. Later records, especially from the late 1800s onward, are much richer. A full marriage record may show:

  • Both full names, including the bride's maiden name — sometimes the only place it survives
  • Ages or birth dates and birthplaces for bride and groom
  • Parents' names on later records — the single most valuable item for extending a tree
  • Residences and occupations, and whether either party had been married before
  • Witnesses, who are frequently siblings, parents, or in-laws
  • The officiant, whose church affiliation can point you to church records when civil ones fail

Where to search, in order

Start with the online indexes: the large record sites and many state archives have indexed county marriages, and an index hit gives you the county, date, and often a book and page number. Next, the county courthouse itself — the clerk of court or register's office holds the original books, and many will copy a record for a small fee if you can give names and an approximate date. State archives hold microfilm or transferred records for many counties, which is also where to check when a courthouse has suffered a fire or flood. Church registers are the parallel track: where civil recording started late or a record is missing, the congregation's own book may be the only copy. These are the same sources our record search services work through every day.

Two honest catches

First: search where the bride lived. Couples usually married in the bride's home county, at her family's church, even if the groom lived elsewhere and the couple settled somewhere else entirely. A marriage that seems to be missing from the county where the family lived is very often sitting in the next county over, under the bride's family's roof. Second: early marriages may have left only a bond or a register line — or, on the frontier and in burned-record counties, nothing civil at all. A date carried in an online tree with no source may rest on nothing more than an estimate from the first child's birth. Treat unsourced marriage dates as Inference, not fact, until a record backs them.

Narrow the date before you search

Clerks and indexes both reward a tight window. Work backward from the first known child's birth, check the couple's ages and residences in the nearest census, and look for the groom in the county a year or two before that birth. A search for "married sometime in the 1840s, somewhere in Virginia" fails; "married 1843–1846, most likely in the bride's home county" is the kind of search that finds records.
