How to Find Military Service Records for an Ancestor

Military service generated paperwork at every step — enlistment, muster rolls, pay, wounds, discharge — and much of it survives. For the genealogist, military records fall into three great families, and knowing which family to ask for is most of the battle: compiled service records, pension files, and draft registration cards. Each lives in a different place and tells a different part of the story.

Compiled service records: the skeleton of a service

For soldiers of the Revolutionary War through the Civil War and into the early 1900s, clerks later gathered a man's original muster rolls, enlistment papers, and prisoner or hospital records into a single file of cards — the compiled service record. It typically shows his unit, rank in and out, and a card-by-card trail of presence or absence at each muster, with notes on sickness, wounds, capture, or death. These records are held by the National Archives, and many are digitized and indexed on the large record sites. One essential exception: Confederate service records sit in a different frame — the compiled records themselves are at the National Archives, but many supporting records, including most Confederate pensions, were state matters and live at state archives, in the state that paid the pension. Search both levels before concluding a man left no file.

Pension files: often the richest records of all

If your ancestor or his widow applied for a pension, ask for the pension file first. Because applicants had to prove service, identity, and family relationships, pension files routinely contain material found nowhere else: discharge papers, affidavits from comrades and neighbors, and — in widows' pensions — proof of marriage, the soldier's death date and place, and the names and birth dates of minor children, sometimes copied from a family Bible register. Invalid (disability) pensions add medical detail across decades. A pension index card will tell you whether a file exists and under what name and application numbers; the full file, ordered from the National Archives for federal pensions or the state archives for Confederate ones, is where the family history is. Draft registration cards: WWI and WWII

For the twentieth century, the most widely surviving records are the draft cards. The World War I registration of 1917–1918 covered men born roughly between 1872 and 1900 and gives birth date and place, occupation, employer, nearest relative, and a physical description. World War II had several registrations; the best known is the Fourth Registration of April 1942 — the "old man's registration" — which covered men born between 1877 and 1897 who were too old for active service but still required to register. Those cards give a birth date and place, residence, employer, and the name of a person who would always know the man's address — often a wife or adult child. Remember what a draft card is: proof a man registered, not proof he served. Treat service itself as unproven until a service record or discharge backs it.

The 1973 fire — and working honestly around it

One catch must be stated plainly. In July 1973, a fire at the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis destroyed a huge share of the Army's personnel files for the period roughly 1912 to 1960 — on the order of 80 percent of them — along with some Air Force records. For many World War I and World War II soldiers, the full service file simply no longer exists, and no archive can produce it. What can still be done: the center can sometimes reconstruct basic service information from auxiliary sources such as pay records and morning reports, and final pay vouchers survived separately for many men. The honest approach is to gather what survives, label each fact with the record it came from, and leave the gap a gap. A burned file is a fact about the archive, not a space to be filled with plausible invention — and any report that reads smoothly across 1912–1960 Army service without naming its sources deserves a second look.
